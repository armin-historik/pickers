History

A leather component for weaving

Mechanical looms required components capable of withstanding repeated impacts. The picker transferred the force of the picking mechanism to the shuttle. Gebrüder Krafft developed its own manufacturing process for these parts.

1860s

According to the Krafft anniversary publication, experiments based on English models began in the early 1860s.

England

The first auxiliary machines developed in-house proved unsuccessful. Krafft obtained equipment from England and enlisted the help of an English specialist.

1913

The surviving company catalogue shows numerous picker designs, as well as protectors and buffers.

An unusual sales story

Made in Fahrnau – sold as English goods

The anniversary publication reports that customers initially preferred English pickers and rejected those made by Krafft. A dealer subsequently sold the Fahrnau-made products as English goods.

When Krafft later took over direct sales, the company first had to explain to customers that the products they had believed to be English had in fact come from Fahrnau. According to the company’s account, sales subsequently developed favourably.

This account comes from the company’s retrospective anniversary publication of 1929. It is a valuable historical source, but does not independently verify every event described.

A small leather component tells a story of technology transfer, perceptions of quality, and competition between German and English industry.

Industrial context

Weaving mills in the Wiesental

During the 19th century, the Wiesental developed into an important textile region. Spinning mills and mechanical weaving mills shaped the communities along the River Wiese. Mechanical looms needed wear-resistant components, including leather pickers.

Textile centres in the region

InZellandSchönau, the Mechanische Weberei Zell and the Spinnerei und Weberei Schönau were important businesses. Haagen bei LörrachandTodtnauwere also part of the regional textile industry. However, not every textile factory operated looms: spinning and weaving mills performed different tasks.

Was Krafft a supplier?

The manufacture of pickers by Gebrüder Krafft is documented in the 1929 anniversary publication and the 1913 company catalogue.Direct deliveries to the weaving mills named here have not yet been documented.

Their geographical proximity nevertheless illustrates the industrial environment in which leather-goods production developed in Fahrnau.

For regional background:Wiesental Textile Museum in Zell.

Materials and production

From buffalo hide to loom picker

According to the anniversary publication, Krafft usedbuffalo hides. These were limed, dehaired and then dried. A neighbouring tannery initially carried out this preparatory work; later it was performed in-house.

The historical account describes the transition from hand presses to mechanical presses. The variety of models in the catalogue also shows that the components were adapted to different types of loom.

Pickers should not be confused withpicking straps: both were used in weaving machinery, but served different purposes.

The Wiesental as an industrial setting

Fahrnau was situated in a region with a substantial textile industry. Krafft’s manufacture of these wear parts illustrates a technical connection between leather processing and mechanical weaving.

Today · Preserving historic machinery

HAAG pickers in working museum looms

Pickers are not confined to historic catalogues. They are still needed in operational shuttle looms, and the heavy mechanical loads they withstand can cause them to break or wear out.

The two comparison pieces documented here bear theHAAGbrand. Their visible model markings read701 A MXXand701 EXX. They are products of a different make from those illustrated in the 1913 Krafft catalogue. No manufacturing connection with Gebrüder Krafft has been established.

When spare parts are scarce

In November 2026, a museum still operating historic weaving machines informed us that it also uses pickers and that one had recently broken. Because suitable replacements have become difficult to obtain, the museum was considering making its own replacement.

Industrial heritage today:Keeping historic machinery running depends on more than preserving the large machines. Small, heavily stressed replacement parts must also remain available or be reproduced appropriately.
